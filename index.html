Model assumptions
  • Real-gas properties: cp(T) for dry air and cp(T, fuel–air ratio) for kerosene combustion products (Walsh & Fletcher polynomials, fitted 200–2000 K, extended linearly to 2200 K). Ideal-gas equation of state; no dissociation.
  • ISA ambient conditions at the chosen altitude.
  • Ideal intake: full ram total pressure recovery.
  • Complete combustion of kerosene, LHV 43.1 MJ/kg. Fuel mass flow is included through the turbine and nozzle.
  • Turbine work equals compressor work (no mechanical losses or bleed).
  • Ideal nozzle, fully expanded to ambient pressure.

User guide

What this tool shows

It models the simplest jet engine, a turbojet. Air comes in and is squeezed by the compressor. Fuel is burned in it, making it very hot. The hot gas spins a turbine, which takes out just enough power to drive the compressor. Everything left over leaves through the nozzle as a fast jet. Because the jet leaves faster than the air came in, the engine pushes the aircraft forward.

How to use it

  • Drag a slider or type a number in its box. Every value and chart updates immediately.
  • Press Ideal cycle to make the compressor, turbine and combustor loss-free. Their sliders are greyed out until you press it again. This shows how much the losses cost.
  • If a combination cannot make a working engine, a red message explains why. A common case is a turbine inlet temperature lower than the compressor exit temperature.
  • In the charts with several curves, only one input is varied and all the others stay at your settings. The black dashed curve and black dot always show your current design.

The numbered points (stations)

0Still air ahead of the engine, at the chosen altitude
2Compressor entry. Forward speed has already slowed and slightly compressed the air (ram effect)
3Compressor exit: highest pressure in the engine
4Turbine entry, just after the combustor: hottest point

9Nozzle exit: the jet leaves at outside air pressure

Reading the T–s diagram

  • Up means hotter. Right means more entropy, which grows when heat is added and when there are losses (friction, mixing).
  • A vertical line is a perfect, loss-free compression or expansion. The dashed lines 2→3s and 4→5s show what perfect components would do.
  • The real lines lean to the right. The lower the component efficiency, the more they lean, and the more work the compressor needs or the less the turbine gives.
  • In the combustor (3→4) temperature climbs along a line of constant pressure (an isobar). A combustor pressure loss moves point 4 onto a slightly lower-pressure isobar, further right.
  • The dashed line from 9 back to 0 is the hot exhaust cooling down in the atmosphere. This closes the cycle; the heat lost here is the main loss of every jet engine.

What the results mean

Thermal efficiencyShare of the fuel energy turned into extra kinetic energy of the gas. Mainly raised by pressure ratio.
Propulsive efficiencyShare of that kinetic energy that actually pushes the aircraft. Highest when the jet is only a little faster than the aircraft.
Overall efficiencyThermal × propulsive: the share of fuel energy that ends up as useful thrust power.
SFCFuel burned per second for each unit of thrust. Lower is better.
Specific thrustThrust per kg/s of air. Higher means a smaller engine can give the same thrust.
Gross thrustPush from the jet leaving the nozzle.
Ram dragDrag from accelerating the incoming air up to the aircraft's speed.
Net thrustGross thrust minus ram drag: the force that actually moves the aircraft.

Where the fuel power goes

The power-flow diagram starts with all the fuel heat (100 %). Part leaves as heat in the hot exhaust. The rest becomes jet power, of which only part becomes thrust power; the remainder is kinetic energy left behind in the jet. The loop shows the turbine power handed back to the compressor. It is large, often more than half the fuel power, but it circulates inside the engine and is not lost.

How the numbers are calculated

Tap a step to open it. The steps run in the same order as the program, and each uses results from the ones before. Values are per kg of air unless stated. R = 287.05 J/(kg·K).

1. Outside air and flight condition

The standard atmosphere (ISA) gives temperature and pressure at altitude h (in metres). Then the speed of sound and Mach number follow.

(1.1)T0 = 288.15 − 0.0065·h (up to 11 000 m)
(1.2)p0 = 101 325·(T0/288.15)5.25588 (up to 11 000 m)
(1.3)Above 11 000 m: T0 = 216.65 K, p0 = 22 632·exp[−g(h − 11 000)/(RT0)], g = 9.80665 m/s²
(1.4)γ0 = cp,a(T0) / [cp,a(T0) − R]
(1.5)a0 = √(γ0RT0), M0 = V0/a0
2. Gas properties that change with temperature

Hot gas stores more energy per degree than cold gas, so specific heat cp is a function of temperature. Combustion products get an extra term that grows with the fuel–air ratio f. Enthalpy h and the entropy function φ are integrals of cp, measured from 298.15 K.

(2.1)z = T/1000
(2.2)cp,a(T) = Σi=0…8 Ai·zi [kJ/(kg·K)], dry air
(2.3)cp,g(T,f) = cp,a + f/(1+f)·Σi=0…7 Bi·zi, kerosene products
(2.4)Above 2000 K: cp,a = cp,a(2000 K) + 0.055·(z − 2), B-term held at its 2000 K value
(2.5)ha(T) = ∫298.15T cp,a dT, hg(T,f) = ha(T) + f/(1+f)·ΔhB(T)
(2.6)φa(T) = ∫298.15T (cp,a/T) dT, φg(T,f) = φa(T) + f/(1+f)·φB(T)
(2.7)s(T,p) = φ(T) − R·ln(p/p0) − φa(T0) (zero at ambient)
(2.8)Isentropic step a→b: φ(Tb) = φ(Ta) + R·ln(pb/pa)
(2.9)Temperature from h or φ (Newton): T ← T − [h(T) − htarget]/cp, T ← T − [φ(T) − φtarget]·T/cp

How this links to the familiar isentropic formula

Equation (2.8) is the isentropic relation, written in the general form that still works when cp changes with temperature. Both versions start from the entropy change of an ideal gas:

(2.10)ds = cp·dT/T − R·dp/p

Isentropic means ds = 0, so cp·dT/T = R·dp/p. Integrating from state 1 to state 2 gives two cases:

  • Constant cp (textbook model): cp comes out of the integral, and with R/cp = (γ−1)/γ:
(2.11)T2/T1 = (p2/p1)(γ−1)/γ
  • cp varying with T (this tool): cp stays inside the integral. That integral is the entropy function φ, which gives (2.8) again: φ(T2) − φ(T1) = R·ln(p2/p1). With constant cp, φ = cp·ln T and it collapses exactly to (2.11).

Two equivalent ways to write the real-gas version:

(2.12)Relative pressure (classic air tables): pr(T) = exp[φ(T)/R], p2/p1 = pr(T2)/pr(T1)
(2.13)Effective exponent: T2/T1 = (p2/p1)(γ̄−1)/γ̄, c̄p = [φ(T2) − φ(T1)] / ln(T2/T1), γ̄ = c̄p/(c̄p − R)

Form (2.13) looks exactly like the familiar formula, but γ̄ depends on the temperatures at both ends, so it can only be found by iteration. The state table shows γ̄ for each isentropic step, so you can see how far it is from 1.4.

Why it matters: at the default settings the compressor starts at 319 K with pressure ratio 20. With γ = 1.4, (2.11) gives T3s = 752 K; the real-gas relation gives 734 K, because γ̄ ≈ 1.39. In the turbine the hot combustion products have γ̄ ≈ 1.30, so assuming 1.4 there would misjudge the expansion far more.

3. Intake: ram compression

The incoming air is slowed to nearly zero speed relative to the engine. Its kinetic energy turns into temperature and pressure, with no losses assumed.

(3.1)h2 = ha(T0) + V02/2 → T2 by (2.9)
(3.2)p2 = p0·exp{[φa(T2) − φa(T0)]/R}
(3.2′)Same in familiar form: p2/p0 = (T2/T0)γ̄/(γ̄−1)
4. Compressor

First the exit temperature of a perfect compressor (T3s), then the real one, which needs more work by the factor 1/ηc.

(4.1)φa(T3s) = φa(T2) + R·ln πc → T3s
(4.1′)Same in familiar form: T3s/T2 = πc(γ̄−1)/γ̄, γ̄ from (2.13) over T2…T3s
(4.2)h3 = h2 + [ha(T3s) − h2]/ηc → T3
(4.3)p3 = πc·p2
(4.4)wc = h3 − h2, Pc = ṁ·wc

A working cycle needs T4 > T3.

5. Combustor

Fuel is added until the gas reaches T4. The heat released must equal the rise in the gas's energy content, which fixes the fuel–air ratio. The combustor also costs some pressure.

(5.1)p4 = p3·(1 − Δpb)
(5.2)Energy balance: f·LHV = (1+f)·hg(T4,f) − ha(T3)
(5.3)Solved for f: f = [ha(T4) − ha(T3)] / [LHV − ha(T4) − ΔhB(T4)]
(5.4)Q = f·LHV, ṁf = f·ṁ, LHV = 43.1 MJ/kg
6. Turbine

The turbine takes exactly the compressor work out of the gas (1+f kg per kg of air), which fixes T5. A real turbine needs a bigger pressure drop than a perfect one for the same work.

(6.1)h4 = hg(T4,f)
(6.2)h5 = h4 − wc/(1+f) → T5
(6.3)h5s = h4 − (h4 − h5)/ηt → T5s
(6.4)p5 = p4·exp{[φg(T5s) − φg(T4)]/R}
(6.4′)Same in familiar form: T5s/T4 = (p5/p4)(γ̄−1)/γ̄, γ̄ over T5s…T4 for products

A working cycle needs p5 > p0, otherwise nothing is left to drive the jet.

7. Nozzle

The gas expands back to outside pressure, turning its remaining pressure into speed.

(7.1)φg(T9) = φg(T5) − R·ln(p5/p0) → T9
(7.1′)Same in familiar form: T9/T5 = (p0/p5)(γ̄−1)/γ̄
(7.2)h9 = hg(T9,f)
(7.3)V9 = √[2·(h5 − h9)]
8. Thrust, efficiencies and SFC
(8.1)Specific thrust Fs = (1+f)·V9 − V0
(8.2)Fg = ṁ(1+f)V9, Dram = ṁV0, Fn = Fg − Dram = ṁFs
(8.3)Jet power gain ΔKE = [(1+f)·V92 − V02]/2
(8.4)ηth = ΔKE/Q
(8.5)ηp = FsV0/ΔKE (0 when V0 = 0)
(8.6)η0 = ηth·ηp = FsV0/Q
(8.7)SFC = f/Fs [kg/(N·s)]; ×106 → g/(kN·s); ×3600·9.80665 → lb/(lbf·h)

With fuel mass neglected, (8.5) becomes the familiar 2V0/(V9 + V0).

9. Power-flow diagram

Each band is a power; widths are drawn to scale and percentages are of the fuel heat.

(9.1)Fuel heat Q̇ = ṁ·f·LHV (100 %)
(9.2)Heat lost in exhaust = Q̇ − ṁ·ΔKE
(9.3)Jet power = ṁ·ΔKE
(9.4)Thrust power = Fn·V0
(9.5)Kinetic energy left in jet = ṁ·ΔKE − Fn·V0
(9.6)Turbine → compressor loop = Pc = ṁ·wc
10. Drawing the T–s diagram and the sweep charts

Station entropies come from (2.7), using air for stations 0, 2 and 3 and combustion products for 4, 5 and 9. Each process line is drawn with n = 40 points:

(10.1)Ti = Ta·(Tb/Ta)i/n
(10.2)si = sa + (sb − sa)·[φ(Ti) − φ(Ta)] / [φ(Tb) − φ(Ta)]

This is exact for perfect (vertical) steps, constant-pressure heating and cooling, and real compression or expansion at constant polytropic efficiency ηpol, because then:

(10.3)R·d(ln p) = ηpol·dφ

Guide isobars use (2.7) at constant pressure, with air below T3, a blend between T3 and T4, and products above. The sweep charts repeat steps 1 to 8 for each point, changing only πc or T4.
